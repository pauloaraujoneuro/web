# 01 — cirurgia-nervos-perifericos

## Definition

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Peripheral nerve surgery covers operations intended to restore, preserve or redirect peripheral nerve function after compression, trauma or other nerve injury. Common strategies include decompression/neurolysis, direct repair, nerve grafting, nerve transfer and, in selected reconstructions, tendon transfer. (S10, S11, S12)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] There is no single "peripheral nerve surgery": the procedure depends on location, injury mechanism, continuity of the nerve, time since injury, remaining donor nerves/muscles and evidence of spontaneous recovery. (S10)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Nerve transfer redirects an expendable functioning donor nerve or fascicle to the injured nerve's distal target, often shortening the distance axons must regenerate compared with repairing a very proximal lesion. (S12)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Surgery is only one part of treatment. Observation, protection of joints, pain management, physiotherapy/occupational therapy and motor/sensory retraining can be central parts of care. (S10, S24)

## Who it affects

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Relevant populations include patients with traumatic laceration/stretch injuries, brachial plexus injuries, focal compressive neuropathies, iatrogenic injuries and selected spinal-cord-injury reconstructions. (S10, S12)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Epidemiology varies radically by nerve and mechanism; avoid presenting one generic incidence number for "peripheral nerve injury" as if it represented the whole field.

## Symptoms / natural history

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Symptoms depend on the injured nerve and may include numbness, neuropathic pain, weakness, paralysis, loss of dexterity and autonomic changes.
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Some stretch/compression injuries recover spontaneously; transections, avulsions and severe axonal injuries are less likely to regain useful function without reconstruction. (S10)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Time matters most for motor recovery because chronically denervated muscle progressively loses its ability to be successfully reinnervated. (S11)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] The appropriate observation period therefore differs between neurapraxic injuries, closed traction injuries, transections, avulsions and compressive neuropathies. (S10, S11)

## Red flags / early specialist assessment

- New or progressive motor weakness.
- Major traumatic injury with immediate paralysis or sensory loss.
- Open injury along the course of a major peripheral nerve.
- Associated vascular injury.
- Severe neuropathic pain following trauma with major neurological deficit.
- No expected recovery during serial clinical/electrophysiological follow-up.
- Suspected plexus/root avulsion.

## Diagnosis

- [EXPERT-OPINION] History and detailed motor/sensory examination determine the anatomical level and severity of injury. Serial examinations are important because trajectory of recovery changes management. (S10)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Nerve-conduction studies and EMG can document axonal loss, denervation/reinnervation and localization, but timing after injury affects interpretation.
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Ultrasound and MRI/MR neurography can be useful selectively for nerve continuity, masses, fibrosis and anatomical localization; they do not replace clinical examination.

## Conservative versus surgical management

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Observation is reasonable when spontaneous recovery is biologically plausible and serial examination demonstrates improvement. (S10)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Surgery becomes more relevant with nerve transection, severe injury without meaningful recovery, compressive lesions with persistent/progressive deficit, or when the expected time to reinnervation of a target muscle is becoming unfavorable. (S10, S11)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] There is no universally superior reconstructive technique; direct repair, grafting and transfer solve different anatomical problems. (S11, S12)

## What surgery involves

- Neurolysis/decompression: free a nerve from a constricting structure or scar.
- Direct repair: reconnect cut nerve ends when tension-free approximation is possible.
- Nerve graft: bridge a gap between nerve ends.
- Nerve transfer: connect a functioning donor nerve/fascicle close to an important denervated target.
- Tendon transfer: redirect functioning muscle-tendon units when nerve reconstruction alone is unlikely to restore the required function.

## Recovery

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Nerve recovery is usually measured in months rather than days or weeks.
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Reinnervation time depends strongly on distance between the repair/transfer and target muscle, severity of injury, age, target condition and rehabilitation. (S11, S24)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] After nerve transfers, initial detectable muscle activity may take approximately 2–6 months in some reconstructions, with further strength and motor relearning continuing much longer. This is procedure-specific, not a universal promise. (S24)

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do NOT give a generic "success rate for peripheral nerve surgery."
- Outcome needs to be stated for:
  - exact nerve;
  - injury level;
  - surgical technique;
  - delay from injury;
  - target function;
  - outcome definition such as MRC ≥3 or ≥4.
- Earlier reconstruction is generally associated with better motor outcomes in severe injuries where spontaneous recovery will not occur, but exact timing differs by pathology. (S11)

## FAQ seeds

1. "Todo nervo machucado precisa de cirurgia?"
   - No. Some compression/stretch injuries recover without reconstruction; anatomy, severity and serial recovery matter.

2. "Quanto tempo um nervo demora para se recuperar?"
   - Usually months; time depends on distance to the target and type of injury/repair.

3. "O que é transferência de nervo?"
   - A functioning donor nerve is redirected to an important denervated target closer to the muscle.

4. "Qual é a diferença entre enxerto e transferência de nervo?"
   - Graft bridges the injured nerve pathway; transfer creates a new donor pathway closer to the target.

5. "Fisioterapia ainda é necessária depois da cirurgia?"
   - Yes. Joint protection, strength, sensory work and motor relearning are often integral to functional recovery.

6. "Quanto mais cedo operar, melhor?"
   - Often true for selected severe motor injuries, but operating too early in an injury capable of spontaneous recovery may also be inappropriate.

7. "O nervo volta a ser exatamente como antes?"
   - Avoid promises. Functional recovery varies substantially.

8. "EMG diz se preciso operar?"
   - It contributes to the decision but must be interpreted together with examination, mechanism, imaging where appropriate and serial recovery.

## Terminology

peripheral nerve surgery → cirurgia de nervos periféricos → "cirurgia de nervo"
nerve transfer → transferência nervosa → "transferência de nervo"
nerve graft → enxerto de nervo → "enxerto de nervo"
neurolysis → neurólise → "liberação do nervo"
nerve decompression → descompressão nervosa → "cirurgia para descomprimir o nervo"

## Do not state

- "Nerve surgery restores normal function."
- "Nerve transfer is better than grafting."
- "All nerves regenerate at X mm/day therefore recovery occurs in X months."
- One generic success percentage.
- "Minimally invasive" as a synonym for better outcome without procedure-specific evidence.
- Guaranteed recovery of sensation or strength.

# 02 — lesao-plexo-braquial

## Definition

- [SR/MA] Adult traumatic brachial plexus injury damages the network carrying motor and sensory signals from cervical roots to the upper limb. Severity ranges from stretch injuries capable of spontaneous recovery to rupture or root avulsion. (S13, S14)
- [SR/MA] Serious surgically treated adult injuries are overwhelmingly associated with high-energy trauma. (S14)

## Who it affects

- [SR/MA] In a meta-analysis of 3,032 surgically reconstructed adult patients, 93% were male. Closed injuries represented ~93%. Motorcycle crashes represented ~67% of closed injuries and automobile crashes ~14%. These numbers describe severe surgical cohorts, not all BPI. (S14)
- [COHORT] Brazilian and international cohorts likewise show young men and motorcycle trauma as major populations.

## Natural history

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Stretch injuries may recover spontaneously; ruptures and root avulsions have markedly different potential.
- [SR/MA] Timing review found better motor outcomes when reconstruction of traumatic stretch/blunt injuries occurred within 6 months. A period around 3 months can allow identification of spontaneous recovery while avoiding excessive delay in appropriate candidates. (S13)

## Red flags

- Complete or near-complete paralysis immediately after major shoulder/neck trauma.
- Horner syndrome after traction injury, raising concern for proximal/root-level injury.
- Major vascular injury.
- Open penetrating injury.
- Severe neuropathic/deafferentation pain.
- Diaphragmatic dysfunction or associated chest/neck trauma.
- Failure of expected clinical recovery during early follow-up.

## Diagnosis

- Detailed mapping of strength by individual muscle, sensation and reflexes.
- Serial examination.
- EMG/NCS after appropriate interval to evaluate denervation and reinnervation.
- MRI and selected advanced imaging for root/plexus anatomy.
- CT myelography may still have a role in selected root-avulsion assessment depending on local protocol.
- Assessment must include shoulder/scapular mechanics and donor nerve availability.

## Management

- [SR/MA] For closed stretch/blunt injuries, the central tension is waiting long enough to detect spontaneous recovery without waiting so long that motor reconstruction becomes biologically unfavorable. (S13)
- Serious injuries may use combinations of neurolysis, grafts, nerve transfers, free functional muscle transfer and tendon procedures.
- Rehabilitation begins before surgical decision: maintain passive range of motion and prevent contractures.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- [SR/MA] Martin et al. pooled individual patient data from 569 patients: MRC ≥3 recovery was reported in 89.7% of patients operated within 3 months versus 35.7% with delays >12 months. This is heterogeneous historical evidence and must NOT be turned into a patient-level probability. (S13)
- Goal-specific reporting is preferable: elbow flexion, shoulder abduction/external rotation, hand function, pain.

## FAQ seeds

- "O braço pode voltar sozinho?"
- "Como saber se o nervo arrancou da medula?"
- "Por que não esperar um ano antes de operar?"
- "EMG precisa ser repetido?"
- "O que é transferência de nervo?"
- "Dá para recuperar a mão ou só ombro/cotovelo?"
- "Por que a cirurgia pode usar um nervo que ainda funciona?"
- "Quanto tempo leva para aparecer movimento?"

## Do not state

- All BPI requires surgery.
- Surgery must always occur at exactly 3 months.
- A cited MRC recovery rate = chance of "normal arm".
- MRI alone determines operative candidacy.

# 03 — transferencia-nervosa-tetraplegia

## Definition

- [SR/MA] Upper-limb reconstruction after cervical spinal cord injury can use tendon transfers, nerve transfers, tenodesis, arthrodesis and combinations to restore elbow extension, wrist extension, hand opening, pinch and grasp. (S16-S19)
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Nerve transfer differs conceptually from tendon transfer: it attempts to reinnervate a paralyzed muscle using a functioning donor nerve, whereas tendon transfer redirects force from an already functioning muscle.
- [EXPERT-OPINION] Modern reconstruction increasingly combines nerve and tendon transfers rather than treating them as competing approaches. (S19)

## ICSHT

- [EXPERT-OPINION] International Classification for Surgery of the Hand in Tetraplegia (ICSHT) groups limbs according to sensibility and the number of useful MRC ≥4 muscles below the elbow. (S18, S19)
- Motor groups progress approximately:
  - 0: no useful muscle below elbow
  - 1: brachioradialis
  - 2: ECRL
  - 3: ECRB
  - 4: pronator teres
  - 5: FCR
  - 6: finger extensors
  - 7: thumb extensor
  - 8: partial digital flexors
  - 9: extrinsic digital flexion present; intrinsic deficit remains
- Treat classification as a planning framework, not an automatic operation algorithm.

## Patient selection

- Functional goals matter: independence, transfers, wheelchair use, self-catheterization, feeding, hygiene, work/computer use etc.
- Donor nerve integrity, lower-motor-neuron status, muscle/joint contracture and commitment to prolonged rehabilitation are key.
- Electrodiagnostic testing may identify whether target muscles retain viable lower-motor-neuron connections.

## Management / timing

- [SR/MA] Literature contains successful nerve transfers from several months to years after SCI, but timing is target-dependent and chronic denervation may eliminate some options. (S16, S17)
- Tendon transfers generally remain available later if adequate donor muscles exist.
- Multidisciplinary planning is essential.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- [SR/MA] A 2019 systematic review included 158 transfers in 92 patients; extensor and elbow-extension reconstructions showed more consistent strength recovery than some flexor strategies, but evidence was heterogeneous and dominated by small series. (S16)
- [COHORT] A 2024 multicentre tendon-transfer cohort of 200 arms reported mean 1-year grip strength 5.2 kg and pinch 2.1 kg; outcomes differed by ICSHT group and sex. These numbers belong specifically to that cohort. (S20)
- [COHORT] Do not translate grip/pinch strength into "percentage of hand recovery."

## FAQ

- "Tetraplegia significa que cirurgia não consegue melhorar a mão?"
- "Qual a diferença entre transferência de nervo e de tendão?"
- "A cirurgia cura a lesão medular?"
  - No. It reconstructs selected upper-limb functions; it does not repair the spinal cord itself.
- "Quanto tempo depois da lesão ainda dá para operar?"
- "O que é ICSHT?"
- "Qual movimento costuma ser priorizado?"
- "Vou precisar reaprender a mexer a mão?"
  - Frequently yes, particularly after nerve transfers.
- "Dá para fazer os dois braços?"

## Do not state

- Surgery treats/cures tetraplegia.
- Every cervical SCI is eligible.
- Nerve transfer always avoids tendon transfer.
- Long delays never matter.
- Published strength averages predict an individual patient's result.

# 04 — sindrome-tunel-carpo

## Definition

- [GUIDELINE] CTS is compression of the median nerve beneath the transverse carpal ligament. (S1)
- [GUIDELINE] It is the most common upper-extremity compressive neuropathy. (S1)

## Epidemiology

- [GUIDELINE] AAOS cites annual incidence around 329/100,000 person-years and historical general-adult prevalence 2.7–4.9%. (S1)
- [SR/MA] A newer global meta-analysis found extremely heterogeneous estimates and a pooled prevalence of ~14.4%; methodology and population differences are substantial, so avoid presenting it as a universal prevalence. (S2)

## Symptoms

- Numbness/tingling primarily in median-nerve fingers.
- Nocturnal symptoms and sleep disturbance.
- Pain may radiate proximally.
- Advanced disease can produce impaired thumb opposition, weakness and thenar atrophy.

## Diagnosis

- [GUIDELINE] 2024 AAOS: strong evidence supports CTS-6 as a diagnostic tool instead of routine ultrasonography or routine NCV/EMG. (S1)
- [GUIDELINE] AAOS: MRI should not be used to diagnose routine CTS. (S1)
- Electrodiagnostics remain useful when diagnosis is uncertain, severity/localisation is needed, alternative neuropathy is suspected or surgical planning requires it.

## Management

- Night splinting may provide symptomatic relief in appropriate mild/moderate disease.
- [GUIDELINE] Corticosteroid injection does **not** provide long-term improvement, though this does not mean no short-term benefit. (S1)
- [GUIDELINE] AAOS found a long list of nonoperative interventions without demonstrated long-term patient-reported benefit; avoid implying every advertised modality is evidence-based. (S1)
- Surgery releases the transverse carpal ligament to reduce median-nerve pressure.

## Surgical technique

- [GUIDELINE] Strong evidence: mini-open and endoscopic carpal tunnel release show no difference in patient-reported outcomes. (S1)
- [GUIDELINE] Local anaesthesia alone can be used. (S1)
- [GUIDELINE] Routine postoperative supervised therapy and postoperative splint immobilisation are not recommended for uncomplicated release. (S1)

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do not advertise a single "95% success" number.
- Improvement of nocturnal paresthesia may precede recovery of longstanding numbness or thenar weakness.
- Severe chronic axonal loss may limit neurological recovery despite technically adequate decompression.

## FAQ

- "Preciso fazer eletroneuromiografia?"
- "Digitar muito causa túnel do carpo?"
  - AAOS 2024 states reliable evidence linking high keyboard use to CTS is lacking.
- "Infiltração cura?"
- "Quando é hora de operar?"
- "Cirurgia aberta ou endoscópica é melhor?"
- "Vou precisar usar tala depois?"
- "Quando posso voltar a trabalhar?"
- "A dormência some na hora?"

## Do not state

- Keyboard use definitively causes CTS.
- Endoscopic release is superior.
- Steroid injection is a long-term cure.
- EMG is mandatory for every patient.
- Recovery is immediate in severe chronic CTS.

# 05 — sindrome-cubital

## Definition

- Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow/cubital tunnel syndrome is compression or irritation of the ulnar nerve around the elbow.
- [SR/MA] It is generally regarded as the second most common upper-limb compression neuropathy. A 2020 meta-analysis cites population prevalence around 6%, but definitions vary. (S5)

## Symptoms

- Numbness/tingling involving the small finger and ulnar half of ring finger.
- Symptoms often worsened by sustained elbow flexion or pressure on the medial elbow.
- Advanced disease: intrinsic hand weakness, impaired pinch/finger coordination, muscle wasting and clawing.

## Natural history

- [COHORT] Historical untreated cohort: around half of patients reported symptomatic improvement over one year after education/avoidance of aggravating posture. Small study; do not generalise to advanced disease. (PMID 12464337)
- [RCT] Mild UNE: surgery produced faster improvement at 3 months than conservative care, but no difference at 6–12 months in one RCT. (S6)

## Diagnosis

- Clinical history/exam.
- NCS/EMG can confirm and grade neuropathy/localise lesion.
- [GUIDELINE] AANEM evidence-based guideline recommends offering ultrasound measurement of ulnar nerve cross-sectional area/diameter to confirm/localise UNE (Level B). (S3)

## Conservative management

- Avoid prolonged elbow flexion and direct pressure.
- Night positioning/splinting can be used.
- [SR/MA] Meta-analysis found symptomatic improvement in ~89% of splinted cases, but underlying studies were heterogeneous and many were non-randomised. Do not present as a guaranteed effectiveness rate. (S4)

## Surgery

- In-situ decompression releases constricting structures.
- Transposition moves the nerve anteriorly in selected anatomical/clinical situations.
- [SR/MA] 2020 network meta-analysis: ~87% improved after surgery across included studies, with in-situ decompression showing favourable safety/effectiveness rankings. (S5)
- [SR/MA] 2024 RCT network meta-analysis found no clear response-to-treatment difference among common techniques and highlighted heterogeneity. (PMID 38456175)

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- 87% improvement is a pooled research estimate from selected surgical cohorts, not a patient guarantee. (S5)
- Severe longstanding intrinsic muscle denervation has worse recovery potential.

## FAQ

- "Dormir com o cotovelo dobrado piora?"
- "É o mesmo que túnel do carpo?"
- "Preciso de eletroneuromiografia?"
- "Ultrassom do nervo ajuda?"
- "Quando usar tala?"
- "Quando precisa operar?"
- "O nervo precisa ser mudado de lugar?"
- "A força da mão volta totalmente?"

## Do not state

- Transposition is always superior.
- Every patient needs surgery.
- Splinting has a guaranteed ~89% success rate.
- Normal EMG completely excludes clinically meaningful disease.

# 06 — sindrome-tunel-tarso

## Definition

- [SR/MA] Tarsal tunnel syndrome is compression of the posterior tibial nerve or its branches within the tarsal tunnel at the medial ankle. (S7)
- It can be associated with trauma, biomechanical abnormalities, scar, inflammatory disease, varicosities and space-occupying lesions; many cases are idiopathic. (S7)

## Epidemiology

- [SR/MA] Reliable population incidence/prevalence is not established.
- Do not manufacture prevalence numbers: modern reviews explicitly identify the epidemiological evidence as weak. (S7)

## Symptoms

- Burning, tingling, numbness or pain around medial ankle/plantar foot.
- Symptoms can worsen with prolonged standing/activity.
- Differential diagnosis is broad: plantar fasciitis, S1 radiculopathy, polyneuropathy, Morton neuroma, stress fracture and other foot/ankle pathology.

## Diagnosis

- [SR/MA] There is no accepted diagnostic gold standard. A 2026 systematic review of 82 studies/4,213 patients documented substantial variation in diagnostic criteria. (S8)
- Clinical symptoms + examination are central.
- Tinel testing is commonly used, but not definitive.
- NCS/EMG can support diagnosis and identify competing neurological diagnoses, but sensitivity is imperfect.
- Ultrasound/MRI are particularly useful when a structural cause or mass is suspected.

## Management

- Activity/footwear modification, orthoses, analgesia and rehabilitation are commonly used first.
- Evidence for individual conservative modalities is substantially weaker than for CTS.
- [SR/MA] 2024 scoping review found most surgical evidence to be Level IV case-series data. (S7)
- Surgery generally involves releasing the flexor retinaculum and relevant distal branches when appropriately indicated.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- [SR/MA] 2024 scoping review: excellent/good outcomes were reported in ~75.3% of surgical cases, but the evidence was largely low-level observational data with heterogeneous definitions. (S7)
- Other historical reviews report broad surgical success ranges roughly 44–96%.
- Do **not** put those numbers on the public page without explicit explanation of evidence weakness and Dr. Paulo's approval.

## FAQ

- "Túnel do tarso é igual túnel do carpo, só que no pé?"
- "Dor no calcanhar pode ser do nervo?"
- "Eletroneuromiografia normal descarta?"
- "Ultrassom ou ressonância mostram o problema?"
- "Palmilha ajuda?"
- "Quando operar?"
- "Por que é tão difícil diagnosticar?"
- "Cirurgia sempre resolve a queimação?"

## Do not state

- There is a definitive single diagnostic test.
- Tinel alone establishes diagnosis.
- Surgery has a 90%+ universal cure rate.
- Failed conservative treatment by itself proves TTS.

# 07 — cirurgia-coluna

## Definition

- Spine surgery is a group of procedures addressing selected structural disorders of the cervical, thoracic and lumbar spine.
- Conditions include disc herniation/radiculopathy, spinal stenosis, myelopathy, instability, deformity, fractures, tumours and infections. (S40)
- Surgery can involve decompression, discectomy, fusion/stabilisation, reconstruction and selected motion-preserving procedures.

## Key page message

- [GUIDELINE/EXPERT] Most nonspecific back pain does not require surgery. Surgery is relevant when a specific anatomical diagnosis exists for which an operation has a plausible therapeutic target. (S27, S40)
- "Pain + abnormal MRI = surgery" is incorrect.
- Imaging abnormalities are common and must match symptoms/examination.

## Typical reasons surgical assessment becomes relevant

- Progressive neurological deficit.
- Spinal cord compression/myelopathy.
- Cauda equina syndrome.
- Persistent radicular pain/function loss with concordant imaging after appropriate nonoperative management.
- Unstable or neurologically significant trauma.
- Tumour/infection/deformity/instability in selected cases.

## Diagnosis

- History + neurological exam first.
- Imaging should answer a clinical question rather than be ordered reflexively.
- NICE discourages routine imaging for uncomplicated low-back pain in nonspecialist settings.
- MRI is often preferred for nerve root, disc, spinal cord and soft tissue pathology.
- CT has major roles in fracture/bony assessment and selected surgical planning.

## What surgery involves

- Decompression: enlarge space around neural tissue.
- Discectomy: remove disc material compressing a nerve root.
- Fusion: mechanically join vertebral segments when stabilization is required.
- Instrumentation: screws/rods/cages may support stabilization/fusion.
- Minimally invasive describes the access method; it does **not** represent one single operation and does not automatically imply superior long-term outcome.

## Conservative versus surgery

- Disease-specific decision:
  - lumbar disc herniation → many improve without surgery;
  - tolerable lumbar stenosis → often reasonable to observe initially;
  - moderate/severe DCM → guideline-supported surgical treatment;
  - neurologically intact burst fractures → operative versus nonoperative evidence can conflict.
- This should probably be the conceptual centre of the hub page.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do not publish a "success rate of spine surgery."
- Define outcome by diagnosis:
  - leg pain from disc herniation;
  - walking tolerance from stenosis;
  - prevention/improvement of neurological disability in DCM;
  - stability/alignment/neurological protection after trauma.
- Different operations solve different problems.

## FAQ

- "Toda hérnia de disco precisa operar?"
- "Ter uma ressonância alterada significa que preciso de cirurgia?"
- "Neurocirurgião só trata com cirurgia?"
- "Qual a diferença entre descompressão e artrodese?"
- "Toda cirurgia de coluna coloca parafusos?"
- "Cirurgia minimamente invasiva é sempre melhor?"
- "Quando dor nas costas vira urgência?"
- "Quanto tempo leva para voltar às atividades?"
- "Posso continuar com dor mesmo depois da cirurgia?"
- "Quando pedir uma segunda opinião?"

## Do not state

- Spine surgery = fusion.
- Minimally invasive = objectively superior.
- Surgery cures "back pain" as one disease.
- Abnormal MRI necessarily explains pain.
- One generic recovery timeline.

# 08 — hernia-disco

## Definition

- Lumbar disc herniation occurs when disc material extends beyond its normal boundary and may irritate/compress a lumbar nerve root.
- Sciatica/radiculopathy is the clinically important syndrome when pain and/or neurological symptoms follow the affected root.

## Natural history

- [SR/MA] Disc material frequently resorbs during conservative treatment.
- [SR/MA] 2023 meta-analysis of 2,233 patients found an overall radiological resorption incidence of ~70%; sequestration ~88%, extrusion ~67%, protrusion ~38%, bulge ~13%. Most resorption occurred within six months. (S28)
- Radiological resorption ≠ guaranteed symptom resolution.

## Red flags

- New urinary retention/overflow or loss of bladder control.
- Saddle/perineal anaesthesia.
- New bowel dysfunction associated with neurological symptoms.
- Rapidly progressive or severe motor deficit.
- Bilateral severe neurological symptoms.
- Suspicion for infection, malignancy or major trauma.

## Diagnosis

- Clinical pattern and neurological examination.
- MRI when result is likely to change specialist management or surgery is being considered.
- Avoid treating incidental MRI findings.

## Management

- Most uncomplicated cases begin nonoperatively.
- Maintain appropriate activity rather than prolonged bed rest.
- Analgesic/rehabilitation strategy individually determined.
- [GUIDELINE] NICE: consider spinal decompression when nonsurgical treatment has not improved pain/function and radiological findings are consistent with sciatic symptoms. (S27)
- [GUIDELINE] NICE allows consideration of epidural local anaesthetic + steroid in acute severe sciatica. (S27)

## Surgery

- Common procedure: discectomy/microdiscectomy, removing the compressive fragment while preserving neural structures.
- Fusion is not synonymous with routine first-time disc surgery.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- [RCT/COHORT] SPORT long-term data support substantial improvement in appropriately selected surgical and nonoperative patients; as-treated analyses generally favoured surgery for faster/greater relief, while crossover complicates randomised comparisons. (S29)
- Recent technique comparisons generally do not demonstrate dramatic long-term superiority of one standard discectomy access over another.
- Reherniation is possible after surgery.

## FAQ

- "A hérnia pode desaparecer sozinha?"
- "Hérnia grande sempre precisa operar?"
- "Ressonância feia significa caso grave?"
- "Quanto tempo esperar antes de cirurgia?"
- "Perda de força muda a urgência?"
- "Microdiscectomia remove o disco inteiro?"
- "Hérnia pode voltar?"
- "Artrodese é necessária?"

## Do not state

- "90% of hernias disappear" without specifying morphology/outcome.
- MRI resolution guarantees pain resolution.
- All severe-looking hernias require surgery.
- Microdiscectomy prevents all recurrence.

# 09 — estenose-canal-vertebral

## Definition

- Lumbar spinal stenosis is narrowing that can reduce available space for cauda-equina/nerve-root structures.
- The characteristic symptomatic syndrome may include neurogenic claudication: leg pain/heaviness/numbness or weakness induced by standing/walking and improved by sitting/flexion.

## Epidemiology

- [SR/MA] 2020 meta-analysis found clinically defined prevalence estimates around 11% in general populations, ~25% in primary care and ~29% in secondary care, with high heterogeneity/risk of bias. (S30)
- Radiological stenosis also occurs in asymptomatic people; imaging ≠ symptomatic disease. (S30)

## Natural history

- [COHORT] In 146 moderately symptomatic non-surgical patients followed median 3.3 years, leg pain improved in ~32%, was unchanged in ~55%, worsened in ~13%; only ~7% had the initial nonoperative decision revised to surgery. (S31)
- Therefore "stenosis always progressively closes until paralysis" is not supported.

## Diagnosis

- History: neurogenic claudication, radicular symptoms, walking tolerance.
- Neurologic and vascular assessment.
- MRI generally confirms anatomy after clinical suspicion.
- Consider vascular claudication and hip/peripheral neuropathy differentials.

## Management

- Conservative treatment is reasonable when symptoms are tolerable and there is no urgent neurological indication.
- Exercise/rehabilitation and symptom management may help.
- [GUIDELINE] NICE recommends **against** epidural injections for neurogenic claudication from central canal stenosis. (S27)
- [SR/MA] Comparative surgery-vs-nonsurgery evidence has historically been inconsistent; Cochrane 2016 concluded evidence was insufficient to clearly establish superiority, while individual trials and later analyses have often found greater improvement after decompression in selected patients. (S32)
- Report this conflict rather than pretending consensus is stronger than it is.

## Surgery

- Goal: decompression of neural structures.
- Fusion is added only when the pathology/operation creates or involves instability/deformity; it is not inherent to every decompression.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Avoid universal laminectomy success numbers.
- Walking, leg symptoms, disability and quality of life are better endpoints than "opening the canal."
- Severity, comorbidity, symptom burden and instability influence results.

## FAQ

- "Estenose sempre piora com o tempo?"
- "Por que caminhar dói e sentar melhora?"
- "Ressonância com estenose significa cirurgia?"
- "Infiltração funciona?"
- "O que a descompressão faz?"
- "Preciso de artrodese junto?"
- "Fisioterapia pode ajudar?"
- "Posso esperar se os sintomas forem toleráveis?"

## Do not state

- It inevitably progresses to paralysis.
- Severe MRI stenosis alone mandates surgery.
- All decompressions require fusion.
- Epidural steroid is an evidence-based treatment for central neurogenic claudication under NICE.

# 10 — mielopatia-cervical

## Definition

- [GUIDELINE] Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is neurological dysfunction caused by degenerative cervical spinal-cord compression. (S34)
- It is distinct from isolated neck pain and cervical radiculopathy.

## Symptoms

- Hand clumsiness and loss of fine motor control.
- Difficulty with buttons/writing/objects.
- Gait imbalance or stiffness.
- Limb weakness/numbness.
- Hyperreflexia/pathological reflexes on examination.
- Advanced disease can affect mobility and sphincter function.

## Natural history

- [SR/MA] DCM may remain stable for periods but carries a meaningful cumulative risk of neurological deterioration. (S35)
- [SR/MA] 2024 meta-analysis estimated neurological stability in mild DCM at ~91% at 1 year, ~85% at 2 years and ~75% at 5 years; moderate/severe DCM ~83%, 72% and 55%, respectively. Wide credible intervals apply. (S35)
- Use these numbers only as research context, not an individual prediction.

## Diagnosis

- Clinical evidence of myelopathy + cervical cord compression on MRI.
- MRI abnormalities alone do not equal clinical DCM.
- Examination severity often quantified using mJOA and/or Nurick systems.

## Management

- [GUIDELINE] AO Spine/CSRS guideline:
  - recommend surgery for moderate DCM;
  - recommend surgery for severe DCM;
  - mild DCM: offer surgery OR supervised structured rehabilitation; operate if neurological deterioration occurs and consider surgery if patient fails to improve;
  - asymptomatic cord compression without radiculopathy: do not routinely offer prophylactic surgery; educate and follow clinically;
  - nonmyelopathic compression + radiculopathy carries increased myelopathy risk. (S34)

## Surgery

- Goal is decompression of the spinal cord.
- Anterior or posterior approaches may be chosen according to levels, alignment and anatomy.
- Procedures can include ACDF/corpectomy, laminoplasty, laminectomy ± fusion etc.
- "Best approach" is patient/anatomy specific.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Primary rationale includes halting/preventing further neurological decline as well as potential functional improvement.
- Neurological recovery is variable and incomplete recovery can occur, particularly with severe/longstanding disease.
- Avoid describing decompression as a guarantee that normal spinal-cord function returns.

## FAQ

- "Mielopatia é a mesma coisa que hérnia cervical?"
- "Por que estou derrubando objetos?"
- "Desequilíbrio pode vir do pescoço?"
- "A medula consegue se recuperar?"
- "Preciso operar mesmo tendo pouca dor?"
- "Fisioterapia substitui cirurgia?"
- "O que acontece se eu esperar?"
- "Cirurgia evita piora ou recupera o que já perdi?"

## Do not state

- DCM is simply neck pain.
- Every cervical MRI compression requires surgery.
- Surgery guarantees neurological recovery.
- Mild DCM always requires immediate surgery.

# 11 — fraturas-coluna

## Definition

- Thoracolumbar fractures range from stable compression injuries to burst, tension-band and translational injuries with instability and/or neurological injury.
- [GUIDELINE] AO Spine classification broadly categorises morphology into:
  - A: compression;
  - B: tension-band failure;
  - C: displacement/translation;
  plus neurological status and modifiers. (S38)

## Red flags / emergency context

- Weakness/numbness after trauma.
- Loss of bowel/bladder function.
- Altered perineal sensation.
- Significant spinal tenderness after high-risk trauma.
- Reduced consciousness or distracting injuries preventing reliable examination.
- Suspected unstable fracture.

## Diagnosis

- [GUIDELINE] NICE trauma guidance supports urgent imaging according to mechanism/examination.
- In adults with significant trauma, CT is central for bony spinal assessment.
- [GUIDELINE] If a new spinal-column fracture is identified, NICE recommends imaging the rest of the spinal column. (S37)
- MRI is especially relevant when spinal cord/ligament/disc injury needs assessment.

## Management

- Treatment depends on morphology, neurological status, ligamentous injury, deformity and patient factors.
- Stable fractures can frequently be treated without operation.
- Surgery can involve decompression when indicated and stabilisation/instrumentation to restore/maintain mechanical stability.

## Evidence conflict

- [GUIDELINE] CNS 2019: for neurologically intact thoracolumbar burst fractures, evidence comparing surgery with nonoperative treatment is conflicting/insufficient; provider judgment is recommended. (S39)
- This is a very valuable page point: "burst fracture" alone does not create one automatic treatment pathway.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do not provide a generic chance of paralysis or generic surgical success.
- Outcomes differ dramatically between neurologically intact stable injury and fracture-dislocation with spinal cord injury.

## FAQ

- "Fratura na coluna significa que vou ficar paraplégico?"
- "Toda fratura precisa operar?"
- "O que deixa uma fratura instável?"
- "Por que fazer tomografia da coluna toda?"
- "Quando precisa de ressonância?"
- "Colete substitui cirurgia?"
- "Parafusos ficam para sempre?"
- "Quanto tempo o osso demora para consolidar?"

## Do not state

- Every burst fracture requires fixation.
- Every spinal fracture risks paralysis equally.
- Surgery clearly outperforms nonoperative care in all neurologically intact burst fractures.

# 12 — reabilitacao-neurocirurgica

## Definition

- Rehabilitation after neurological/neurosurgical disease aims at activity and participation, not merely muscle strength or imaging changes.
- It may include physiotherapy, occupational therapy, pain/spasticity treatment, assistive technology, orthoses, gait/upper-limb retraining, sensory rehabilitation and psychosocial/vocational interventions.
- [GUIDELINE] WHO frames neurological rehabilitation as a multidisciplinary package tailored to functional goals. (S21)

## Key principles

- Start from patient's actual functional deficits and goals.
- Prevent secondary complications: stiffness, contracture, weakness, pressure injuries, falls, pain etc.
- Task-specific practice is usually more meaningful than isolated impairment measurements.
- After reconstructive peripheral nerve surgery, rehabilitation changes according to the biological phase of nerve recovery.
- After spinal cord injury, rehabilitation includes independence, transfers, wheelchair function, upper-limb function, bowel/bladder management and community participation.

## Peripheral nerve-specific rehabilitation

- Maintain passive joint movement while denervated muscles recover.
- Protect weak/insensate tissues.
- Detect early reinnervation.
- Progress from donor-activation strategies to selective independent movement after nerve transfer. (S24)
- Sensory retraining may be incorporated where appropriate.

## Evidence limitations

- "Neurorehabilitation" is too broad for a single success rate.
- Protocol and intensity must be matched to diagnosis and surgical procedure.

## FAQ

- "Quando começa a fisioterapia?"
- "Reabilitação pode começar antes da cirurgia?"
- "Quanto tempo dura?"
- "Fisioterapia faz o nervo crescer mais rápido?"
- "Qual a diferença entre fisioterapia e terapia ocupacional?"
- "Vou precisar fazer exercícios em casa?"
- "Dor significa que devo parar?"
- "Como sabemos se o nervo começou a funcionar?"

## Do not state

- One universal protocol.
- Rehabilitation guarantees recovery.
- More exercise is always better.
- Electrical stimulation has proven universal regenerative benefit in humans.

# 13 — reeducacao-cortical-biofeedback

## Definition

- Peripheral nerve injury/reconstruction changes the sensory and motor signals reaching the brain.
- Motor and sensory rehabilitation therefore includes not only peripheral tissue recovery but neural relearning/plasticity. (S24)
- After a nerve transfer, the original cortical command for the donor nerve initially activates the new recipient target; rehabilitation aims to develop selective control.

## Motor relearning

- [EXPERT-OPINION] Structured nerve-transfer rehabilitation commonly progresses through:
  1. maintaining cortical representation/visualisation before reinnervation;
  2. identifying first reinnervation, sometimes with surface EMG;
  3. activating the target using the donor movement;
  4. multimodal feedback;
  5. separation of donor and recipient movement;
  6. strengthening and functional task practice. (S24)

## Sensory relearning

- [SR/MA] 2021 systematic review of 16 controlled studies found only four high-quality studies and concluded evidence for conventional sensory re-education was not strong. Early mirror visual feedback and some combined strategies were promising. (S22)
- [RCT] Mirror-based early sensory re-education has been studied after median/ulnar nerve repair, but sample sizes are small.
- Absence of robust evidence should be explicit on the page.

## EMG biofeedback

- [SR/MA] 2018 integrative review found only nine eligible PNI studies with heterogeneous populations and generally low methodological quality; evidence for EMG biofeedback effectiveness was limited. (S23)
- EMG biofeedback can nonetheless be used clinically as a way to expose weak muscle activation to the patient during motor relearning; this is different from claiming that it accelerates nerve regeneration.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do not give an efficacy percentage.
- Evidence is protocol-, injury- and endpoint-specific.
- Main clinically plausible target is improved motor/sensory learning and control, not direct regeneration of the nerve.

## FAQ

- "O cérebro precisa reaprender a mexer o músculo?"
- "Por que o movimento novo depende de outro movimento no começo?"
- "O que é biofeedback por EMG?"
- "Biofeedback faz o nervo crescer?"
  - Evidence does not establish that.
- "Terapia do espelho funciona?"
- "Quanto tempo leva para separar os movimentos?"
- "Posso treinar em casa?"
- "Plasticidade cerebral significa que qualquer função pode voltar?"

## Do not state

- Cortical training regrows nerves.
- EMG biofeedback is proven to improve all nerve injuries.
- Mirror therapy has established strong evidence across all peripheral nerve repairs.
- Neuroplasticity removes peripheral biological limitations.

# 14 — orteses-dinamicas

## Definition

- Dynamic orthoses use elastic/spring or other controlled forces to assist or resist motion while protecting tissues.
- They are used in selected tendon-repair, tendon-transfer and nerve-palsy rehabilitation protocols.
- Static, dynamic and relative-motion designs have different goals; "dynamic orthosis" is not one standard device.

## Purpose

- Protect a repair.
- Maintain desired joint alignment.
- Prevent contracture.
- Assist movement when muscle power is insufficient.
- Permit controlled tendon excursion.
- Balance protection against adhesion/stiffness.

## Evidence

- [SR/MA] Systematic review of extensor tendon zones IV–VIII found early-motion protocols generally provided faster early motion recovery than static immobilisation; heterogeneity prevented declaring one universal best protocol. (S25)
- [SR/MA] Earlier controlled-trial review similarly found early active/dynamic mobilisation produced faster early motion than static immobilisation, while long-term differences narrowed. (S26)
- These tendon-repair data should **not** automatically be extrapolated to every nerve-transfer or tendon-transfer reconstruction.
- Postoperative orthosis choice is procedure-specific and must follow the surgeon/hand therapist protocol.

## Safety / red flags

- New deformity or loss of an actively restored movement.
- Sudden pain/pop after tendon reconstruction.
- Skin pressure injury.
- Excessive swelling.
- Numbness/vascular colour change.
- Orthosis causing progressively increasing neurological symptoms.

## Outcomes

⚠ VERIFY WITH PHYSICIAN

- Do not say dynamic splints "speed healing."
- Faster early range-of-motion in tendon literature does not necessarily mean better final function.
- The desired endpoint may be protection, controlled excursion or functional assistance rather than permanent dependence on the orthosis.

## FAQ

- "Qual a diferença entre órtese dinâmica e tala comum?"
- "Preciso usar o dia inteiro?"
- "Posso tirar para tomar banho?"
- "A órtese faz o músculo voltar a funcionar?"
- "Por que ela usa elásticos?"
- "Se apertar ou formigar é normal?"
- "Quanto tempo vou usar?"
- "Cada cirurgia usa a mesma órtese?"

## Do not state

- Dynamic orthoses are superior in all hand rehabilitation.
- They accelerate biological nerve regeneration.
- One wearing schedule applies to every reconstruction.
- More tension = better rehabilitation.

# Cross-page editorial rules

## Evidence language

Use:

- "may"
- "can"
- "is associated with"
- "guidelines recommend..."
- "evidence suggests..."
- "in selected patients..."
- "depends on..."

Avoid:

- "guarantees"
- "eliminates"
- "definitively"
- "best technique"
- "permanent cure"
- "100% safe"
- "normal function returns"

## Outcomes

Every numerical outcome should carry internally:

- diagnosis
- intervention
- cohort/study type
- N
- follow-up
- exact endpoint
- uncertainty
- physician verification

A number such as "87% improved" must never become "87% success rate"
without explaining how the study defined improvement.

## Imaging

Repeated message across the spine pages:

> A radiological abnormality should be interpreted together with symptoms
> and neurological examination.

Do not frame MRI as the diagnosis by itself.

## CFM / medical advertising

Current rule reference: Resolução CFM nº 2.336/2023.

Important correction to the old assumption:

- Before/after imagery is not categorically prohibited.
- Educational use has explicit conditions.
- Images cannot be manipulated/improved.
- Patient anonymity and privacy requirements apply.
- Demonstration of outcomes should include indications, influencing factors,
  unsatisfactory outcomes and possible complications where applicable.
- Avoid promises of result.
- Avoid claims of superiority.
- Avoid sensationalism.
- Avoid suggesting a technique is exclusive to Dr. Paulo.
- Ensure CRM and RQE presentation follows current CFM requirements.

For this project the simplest editorial policy can still be stricter than the
legal maximum: e.g. choose not to use before/after imagery at all.
